# Data Processing Agreement (DPA)

**between Consenter and the customer (website provider)** **June 2026**

Law & Innovation Technology GmbH (hereinafter 'L\&I') operates a trust service through which data protection-related decisions can be requested or made, and in particular where informed consent can be obtained or given (hereinafter referred to as 'Consenter').

Consenter was developed on the basis of an interdisciplinary research and development process, the aim of which is to incorporate and further develop the state of the art for the effective implementation of informed consent in accordance with Articles 25 and 32 of the GDPR. Insofar as technically possible and economically reasonable, the design of Consenter is based on empirically proven methods for increasing the effectiveness of transparency, consent and control mechanisms.

Consenter comprises the Consenter Cookie Banner, which customers can configure via the Consenter Manager and which can communicate with the Consenter Consent Agent via a technical API and the so-called transfer notification, and the Risk Assessment Centre (see the individual components of the service on the website [www.consenter.eu/de](http://www.consenter.eu/de)). To this end, Consenter processes the personal data of the client's end users on behalf of the client (hereinafter the 'Data Controller'). This Data Processing Agreement sets out the conditions under which Consenter is permitted to process personal data on behalf of the client.

## Part I: EU Standard Contractual Clauses (2021/915)

### SECTION I

#### Clause 1 — Purpose and scope

**(a)** The purpose of these Standard Contractual Clauses (the Clauses) is to ensure compliance with Article 28(3) and (4) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) and/or Article 29(3) and (4) of Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2018 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by the Union institutions, bodies, offices and agencies and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Regulation (EC) No 45/2001 and Decision No 1247/2002/EC.

**(b)** The controllers and processors listed in Annex I have agreed to these Clauses in order to ensure compliance with Article 28(3) and (4) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and/or Article 29(3) and (4) of Regulation (EU) 2018/1725.

**(c)** These Clauses apply to the processing of personal data as specified in Annex II.

**(d)** Annexes I to IV are an integral part of the Clauses.

**(e)** These Clauses are without prejudice to obligations to which the controller is subject by virtue of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and/or Regulation (EU) 2018/1725.

**(f)** These Clauses do not by themselves ensure compliance with obligations related to international transfers in accordance with Chapter V of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and/or Regulation (EU) 2018/1725.

#### Clause 2 — Invariability of the Clauses

**(a)** The Parties undertake not to modify the Clauses, except for adding information to the Annexes or updating information in them.

**(b)** This does not prevent the Parties from including the standard contractual clauses laid down in these Clauses in a broader contract, or from adding other clauses or additional safeguards provided that they do not directly or indirectly contradict the Clauses or detract from the fundamental rights or freedoms of data subjects.

#### Clause 3 — Interpretation

**(a)** Where these Clauses use the terms defined in Regulation (EU) 2016/679 or Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 respectively, those terms shall have the same meaning as in that Regulation.

**(b)** These Clauses shall be read and interpreted in the light of the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 or Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 respectively.

**(c)** These Clauses shall not be interpreted in a way that runs counter to the rights and obligations provided for in Regulation (EU) 2016/679 / Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 or in a way that prejudices the fundamental rights or freedoms of the data subjects.

#### Clause 4 — Hierarchy

In the event of a contradiction between these Clauses and the provisions of related agreements between the Parties existing at the time when these Clauses are agreed or entered into thereafter, these Clauses shall prevail.

#### Clause 5 (Optional) — Docking clause

**(a)** Any entity that is not a Party to these Clauses may, with the agreement of all the Parties, accede to these Clauses at any time as a controller or a processor by completing the Annexes and signing Annex I.

**(b)** Once the Annexes in (a) are completed and signed, the acceding entity shall be treated as a Party to these Clauses and have the rights and obligations of a controller or a processor, in accordance with its designation in Annex I.

**(c)** The acceding entity shall have no rights or obligations resulting from these Clauses from the period prior to becoming a Party.

**(c)** its likely consequences and the measures taken or proposed to be taken to address the breach, including to mitigate its possible adverse effects.

## Annex II: Description of the processing

### 2\. How consent data is processed in an end-user's browser storage

The storage of consent data in the end user's browser storage serves the purpose of automatically transferring and applying previously granted or withheld consents when the end user visits the data controller's website again.

**2.1 Personal data collected**

Consent records constitute personal data insofar as they document an end user's consent.

- Contains a unique consent ID for the consent record (NOT a user ID\!).  
- Contains a timestamp indicating when the consent record was created.  
- If an existing consent record is amended, a new consent record is created, which then contains the ID of a previous consent record that is now out of date.  
- They are weakly identifying, as the presentation of the same consent record (consent ID or timestamp) on two occasions makes it possible to recognise an end user between two visits to a website; however, the consent records themselves provide only very limited insights into the end user's private life.  
- If all purposes have been declined, the consent record (browser storage) contains neither a consent ID nor a timestamp. In this case, the consent record (browser storage) is non-identifying. For technical reasons, however, it is protected in exactly the same way as the weakly identifying versions.

**2.2 How the data is processed and stored**

- Consent records are stored in the end user's browser storage.  
- They are transmitted in encrypted form between the consent banner and the website.  
- SameSite=strict, i.e. the consent record is not automatically sent to third-party websites.  
- The consent record is origin-bound, meaning that only the controller's website can access it.  
- The consent record can be read by JavaScript, as this is technically necessary for the service. However, JavaScript outside the data controller's website does not have access to the consent record. Only the data controller's website can access the consent record.

**2.3 How long the data is stored**

Consent records are stored until users erase it from their browser storage. Consent records can always be deleted by cleaning the browser storage. 

### 3\. How an end user's consent record is processed in the controller's consent store

The data controller also requires access to an end user's consents in order to be able to demonstrate the lawfulness of the processing of their personal data. To this end, L\&I records all consent records (note: these consents contain only consent IDs, NOT user IDs, and cannot be linked to one another).

**3.1 Personal data collected**

Information on the nature of the consent records as personal data can be found in the previous section. Please also note the following specific points:

It is not possible to link the consent records (customer storage) to user profiles, as NO user IDs or quasi-identifiers are stored that would allow such a link. For reasons of data minimisation, NO refusals of consent are stored either.

**3.2 How the data is processed and stored**

Consent records (customer storage) are currently stored centrally in the cloud at L\&I as part of the consent record storage (customer storage) and are protected against unauthorised access through technical and organisational measures, thereby minimising the risk of misuse. Amazon Web Services (AWS) is currently used as a sub-processor for this purpose.

**3.3 How long the data is retained**

The consent records are stored for as long as the data controller needs them to be able to demonstrate the lawfulness of the processing of its end users' personal data.

### 4\. How the end user's default settings and consents are processed if they are a user of the Consenter Consent Agent

If the data controller's end user is a user of the Consenter consent agent, they can pre-set the purposes for which they consent to the processing of their data when visiting the data controller's website or another website via their Consenter consent agent. When an end user visits the data controller's website using their Consenter Consent Agent, their agent offers them the option to adjust their settings to match the data protection standards of the data controller's website; if the end user takes no action, their agent sends their settings to the data controller's website after a short delay. If an end user grants or amends their consent via the data controller's consent banner, this setting is sent back to the end user's consent agent. When an end user visits the data controller's website again, the end user's agent resends their previous consent to the data controller's consent banner to enable a comparison with the user's previous decisions.

**4.1 Personal data collected**

An end user's consent preferences contain information about the end user insofar as they reflect their preferences regarding consent.

However, according to current assessments, they are not considered to be identifying (not even weakly identifying), as the consent preset does not contain a unique identifier nor does it contain sufficient distinctive characteristics that could function as quasi-identifiers.

When an end user grants or changes their consent in the consent banner, this setting is sent back to their consent agent. When an end user visits the controller's website again, their consent agent resends their previous consent to the controller's consent banner.

**4.2 How the data is processed and stored**

A user's default consent options are treated in a similar way under data protection law to the 'no photography' badge that conference attendees can pin to the lapel of their jacket:

- The pre-set consent options are, in effect, public, but this is in the end user's best interests. A user of the Consenter consent agent, for example, wishes to inform every website they visit that they do not wish to share any data with it, or that they would prefer to see personalised adverts rather than contextual banner adverts.  
- As soon as the data controller receives an end user's settings, they process all settings as described in points 2 and 3 above.

### 6\. How anonymised data is processed for analysis purposes

L\&I processes anonymised data for the continuous improvement and further development of the service, as well as for scientific research purposes relating to effective consent processes and the rights of data subjects. For this purpose, L\&I uses Matomo and applies the guidelines published by the French data protection authority, the CNIL (exemption for measuring visitor numbers – configuration guide for Matomo Analytics). ## Annex III: Technical and organisational measures, including those to ensure data security

### Clarification regarding the function and meaning of the term 'state of the art' (see Part 1, Clause 3(a))

In accordance with Articles 25 and 32 of the GDPR, controllers must take account of the state of the art when implementing data protection regulations. With regard to informed consent, the following legal requirements apply.

**1\. Legal requirements for informed consent**

Article 6(1)(a) of the GDPR requires informed consent as one of the possible legal bases for the processing of personal data. According to Article 4(11) of the GDPR, consent is "any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which he or she, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, signifies agreement to the processing of personal data relating to him or her".

Article 25 of the GDPR sets out further requirements for the implementation of informed consent. In particular, a website operator must implement informed consent through technical and organisational measures in such a way that protection against the risks posed by data processing to the fundamental rights of data subjects is effective.

**2\. Demonstration of effectiveness and consideration of the state of the art**

In this context, the state of the art must be taken into account; that is to say, the most effective implementation of informed consent currently available on the market ("best available technology"). The state of the art represents a so-called dynamic reference to the current stage of development. Conceptually, the state of the art demands more than the so-called recognised rules of the art (best practice), because it requires scientific evidence that the implementation in question is indeed the most effective. On the other hand, the state of the art requires less than the so-called state of the art in science, because it does not require the implementation of every concept that has been scientifically proven to be more effective, but only one that is already available on the market. When implementing the state of the art, the responsible body may take the implementation costs into account. This means that it is not obliged to implement the state of the art if the costs involved are disproportionate.

Determining the state of the art requires suitable methods with which the effectiveness of the implementation of informed consent – and thus also the most effective implementation – can be measured and demonstrated. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarifies in its Guidelines 4/2019 on Article 25: Data Protection by Design and by Default that demonstrating effectiveness is the central element of Article 25 of the GDPR. It states:

> "To this end, the controller may define appropriate key performance indicators (KPIs) to demonstrate effectiveness. A key performance indicator is a measurable value chosen by the controller that demonstrates how effectively the controller is achieving its data protection objective. Key performance indicators may be quantitative in nature, such as the percentage of false alarms or false negatives, a reduction in complaints, or a reduction in response times when data subjects exercise their rights; or they may be qualitative in nature, such as performance assessments, the use of rating scales or expert opinions. As an alternative to KPIs, data controllers may demonstrate the effective implementation of the principles by setting out the reasons for their assessment of the effectiveness of the measures and safeguards chosen."

Over the past 10 years, the academic community – drawing on a variety of disciplinary perspectives, ranging from user experience and user interface design to research in human–computer interaction, psychology, behavioural economics and the economics of innovation – has developed a diverse set of methods for measuring and demonstrating the effectiveness of informed consent implementation.

**3\. Practical problem of insufficient information**

In practice, the greatest challenge in implementing effective consent currently lies in ensuring that consent is informed and that the expression of will is unambiguous. To date, evidence of effectiveness has scarcely been provided in practice. On the contrary, there are numerous empirical studies demonstrating that conventional cookie banners do not provide internet users with sufficient information and do not sufficiently enable them to make an informed and unambiguous decision regarding the respective processing purposes on the basis of this information. According to these studies, whilst consumers do prefer data protection-friendly services to those with higher risks, they are unable to recognise them and are therefore unable to opt for them. Furthermore, due to 'consent fatigue', current cookie banners are clicked away so reflexively that one can hardly assume an 'unambiguous expression of will' has been given for the individual processing purposes.

Consequently, current cookie banners generally do not reflect the state of the art within the meaning of Articles 25 and 32 of the GDPR, but at most recognised technical standards (so-called 'best practice'). These best practice rules differ from the state of the art in that the controller is not required to provide empirical evidence of effectiveness. However, best practice does not meet the standard required by Article 25 of the GDPR, which demands proof of effectiveness.

There are numerous academic and practical contributions detailing how consent processes should not be designed. Even where positive guidelines are provided, these merely represent best-practice rules, but offer no empirical evidence that they facilitate effective decision-making processes. A quantitative study, for example, has shown the opposite: that internet users click away from cookie banners – even those designed in accordance with best practice guidelines – in such an uninformed manner that one can hardly speak of 'informed' consent within the meaning of the law.

**4\. Establishing and further developing the state of the art through the Consenter research demonstrator**

The Consenter research demonstrator aims to make the most effective techniques for implementing informed consent – as proven in academic research – available on the market, so that they can be regarded as state of the art in accordance with Articles 25 and 32 of the GDPR.

The following sub-chapter summarises the current state of development, demonstrating the most effective technique in each case. As these techniques can be implemented cost-effectively, their application is generally not hindered by disproportionate costs.

**5\. Open Access: Implementation and further development of the state of the art independently of Consenter**

Even though Consenter plays a key role in the development of the state of the art, data controllers may apply the relevant state of the art independently of Consenter and further develop it themselves. To this end, the research group associated with Consenter makes all concepts, methods, specifications and templates freely available. Reference is made to the following documents, each of which contains further sources:

- UX Design and Methods Toolbox, available at [www.hiig.de/](http://www.hiig.de/)  
- Technical specification for a universal consent standard (ConStand), available at [www.tinyurl.com/ConStand](http://www.tinyurl.com/ConStand) (continuously updated in line with feedback from practice, research, and legislation and enforcement)

### Technical and organisational measures taking into account the state of the art in accordance with Article 25 of the GDPR in conjunction with Article 28(1)

*(see Part 1, Clause 8(c)(1) and Part 2 of the AVV)*

**1\. Informed consent, Articles 5, 6 and 7 in conjunction with Article 25 of the GDPR**

Existing cookie banners designed in accordance with best practice guidelines do not provide sufficient information. The following techniques enable consent mechanisms to be designed in a considerably more informative manner than under current best practice guidelines. The following techniques represent the new state of the art:

- **Specification of purpose:** Website operators must specify their processing purposes in such a way that visitors to their website can recognise the benefits and risks of data processing for their fundamental rights (as internet users always weigh up benefits against risks when making decisions).  
- **Configuration of technologies:** As the specific configuration of the service has a direct impact on the fundamental rights risks faced by website visitors, the website operator must communicate the specific risks arising from that configuration (see below).  
- **Information organisation, layout and visualisations:** Website operators must organise information across multiple levels according to its relevance to users, so as not to overwhelm visitors to their website; the most important information – particularly purposes, benefits and risks – should be presented first, in descending order of importance. Privacy icons and other visualisations should be used for this purpose.  
- **Incorporation of consent agents:** Website operators must incorporate signals from consent agents, as their integration significantly enhances the level of informed consent. Obtaining or providing informed consent with the aid of an agent is significantly more informed and therefore more effective than without an agent.

>   
> **Side note:** The integration of consent agents is provided for in Section 26 of the TDDDG and is currently also being proposed at EU level. As consent mechanisms that incorporate agents are significantly more informed than those without an agent, and agent-supported mechanisms are now available on the market, agent-based consent represents the new state of the art. An obligation to incorporate agents therefore already arises from Article 25 of the GDPR.

**2\. Data minimisation, Article 5 in conjunction with Article 25 of the GDPR**

Many website operators rely on third-party technologies to process their visitors' personal data. In doing so, they often choose the best-known technologies and use them with their default settings. This frequently constitutes a breach of the principle of data minimisation, because the default settings result in more data being processed to a greater extent than website operators actually need. Instead, website operators must check what the technology being used actually does and whether the processing can be restricted ("Know Your Tools"). As this follows directly from Article 5 of the GDPR, the following points serve more to clarify the legal requirements than to outline the current state of the art. However, as they constitute a necessary prerequisite for being up to date with the state of the art in terms of awareness (see previous point), they are explicitly highlighted here:

- **Adjusting the configuration:** When website operators use third-party technologies, they must adjust the default settings to suit their actual needs. The default configuration is often very broad and processes more data than website operators generally require. The correct configuration forms the basis for effectively communicating the risks to fundamental rights (see above).  
- **The technology provider's advertising purposes:** Some third-party providers also process data for their own advertising purposes. In this case, website operators must inform their visitors of this and obtain informed consent for this specific purpose. A general reference to the third-party provider's privacy policy, without specifying its processing purposes, is not sufficient.  
- **Alternative technologies:** Website operators must assess whether they can use a technology that poses fewer risks to their visitors, provided that the technology posing greater privacy risks is not necessary. The fact that a technology posing greater privacy risks is the most widely used does not constitute a necessity.

>   
> **Side note:** Apart from the effective implementation of the data minimisation principle, by choosing a more privacy-friendly technology and configuring it in a manner that complies with data protection regulations, website operators can not only build greater trust among their visitors but also achieve higher consent rates.

**3\. Ability to exercise control, Articles 5, 6 and 7 in conjunction with Article 25 of the GDPR**

Agent-based consent mechanisms not only significantly increase the level of informed consent but also enhance data subjects' ability to manage risks. The following techniques therefore represent the new state of the art:

- **Granular decisions:** With the help of a consent agent, website visitors can make significantly more informed and unambiguous decisions regarding the disclosure of their data.  
- **Adapting to system changes:** When website visitors make a decision, this must be respected on subsequent visits; their ability to process information must not be disrupted by the repeated display of a banner, as this increases 'consent fatigue' and thus leads to less informed decisions.

The use of consent agents is superior to the use of cookies in that the former are persistent and 'remember' website visitors' decisions for as long as they use the agent. Cookies that store users' decisions, on the other hand, are regularly deleted, with the result that the cookie banner reappears on a subsequent visit, even though the user has already made a decision.

Returning visitors must and may only be informed of changes to the website operator's system that alter the data protection risk, as this risk is relevant to the decisions made by data subjects. Where the basis for decision-making changes, the means of control must be adapted accordingly:

- If consent has already been given and the risk to the same fundamental rights subsequently increases, at the very least a notice of the increased risk is required, coupled with the immediate option to withdraw consent.  
    
- If the user did not give consent for a specific purpose during their last visit and the website operator has since been able to reduce the risk, the operator may, conversely, ask for consent again for that purpose during a subsequent visit, as there is now a new basis for decision-making in this instance as well.  
    
- **Proof of consent:** Website operators must provide visitors with proof of whether and for what purposes they have obtained consent. Integrating a consent management tool is a suitable way of doing this, as it provides the tool's users with a centralised and automated overview of to whom they have granted which consents. Equally effective alternatives are also permissible (see, for example, ISO/IEC TS 27560:2023 – Consent Records and Receipts).

>   
> **Side note:** By using these techniques, website operators can achieve greater trust – and thus higher consent rates – not only in the short term but also in the long term across multiple repeat visits.

**4\. Documentation requirements and signal integrity of consent, Articles 5, 6 and 7 in conjunction with Article 32 of the GDPR**

Website operators must be able to demonstrate that consent has been validly obtained and that legal requirements have been met. In this respect, the current state of the art includes the traceability of the chosen configuration as well as the information provided, including the stated benefits and risks.

A further development in the state of the art is also the assurance of signal integrity: website operators must store consents in such a way that they can be verifiably proven not to have been altered. As a rule, this must be done using a signature procedure. If website visitors use a consent agent, all copies of the consent must be secured using the same signature procedure. The shared signature ensures that the consents stored at the various locations (i.e. on the website operator's side and on the website visitor's side) are synchronised.

### Technical and organisational measures taking into account the state of the art in accordance with Article 28(3) and Article 32 of the GDPR

*(see Part 1, Clauses 7.4 and 8 of the AVV)*

**1\. Ensuring confidentiality (Article 32(1)(b) of the GDPR)**

Law & Innovation takes measures to implement the confidentiality requirement. These include, amongst other things, measures for physical access control, electronic access control and internal access control. The technical and organisational measures taken in this context are intended to ensure an appropriate level of security for personal data, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage.

*Physical access control*

- Where personal data is processed, it is stored in secure systems.  
- All systems and devices are updated at regular intervals (software updates).  
- All systems are regularly checked for vulnerabilities.  
- There is no critical IT infrastructure (server systems) on the premises of Law & Innovation.  
- Nevertheless, physical access to the office premises is protected by security measures as far as possible. These include:  
  - Access to the office is restricted to staff and service providers (e.g. cleaning staff) with personalised door transponders/lock cylinders, and the issue and return of keys/transponders are logged.  
  - Visitors must ring the bell, register in person, show identification and are not permitted to move freely around the premises.

*Electronic access control*

- Access to personal data is restricted to a limited group of staff, requires the use of assigned login details (username and password) and takes place exclusively via encrypted channels (HTTPS, TLS/SSL).  
- Group accounts/system logins are restricted to specific applications.  
- Separate user IDs for privileged access.  
- Usernames are immediately deactivated or deleted when staff leave the company.  
- Passwords are not stored in plain text or transmitted unencrypted.  
- The following password requirements apply to user authentication: at least 12 characters; 3–4 character types must be used; upper and lower case letters; no common terms; the password must be changed immediately if there is any reason or indication of misuse; temporary passwords must be updated by the user immediately after account activation.  
- Wherever possible, two-factor authentication is used.  
- Session management.  
- Internal IT security policies.  
- Automatic locking of clients (e.g. staff workstations) after a specified period of inactivity (including password-protected screensavers or automatic sleep mode).

*Internal access control*

- Access is granted in accordance with an authorisation policy and a cryptographic policy.  
- Use of a user and user group management system, as well as access rights management.  
- SSH is disabled wherever possible.  
- Authorisations are assigned on a tiered basis depending on the employee's area of responsibility. The principle of least privilege always applies.

*Further measures*

- Strict segregation of duties: Data is not processed jointly for different processing purposes. This involves supporting (logical or physical) client segregation or functional segregation.  
- When the relevant purpose of data processing ceases to apply, the data is deleted. This is carried out in accordance with the data deletion policy.  
- Encryption of data at rest.

**2\. Ensuring integrity (Article 32(1)(b) of the GDPR)**

Measures are taken to ensure integrity. These include, amongst other things, input control measures, as well as measures that generally help to protect against unauthorised or unlawful processing, destruction or accidental damage.

*Transmission control*

Measures to ensure that personal data cannot be read, copied, altered or removed by unauthorised persons during electronic transmission, transport or storage on data media, and that it is possible to verify and determine to which recipients personal data is to be transmitted via data transmission facilities:

- Data transmission is encrypted.  
- Data encryption is always used when transmitting data over the internet.  
- Only secure wireless networks (Wi-Fi) are used, all of which are encrypted using WPA-2.  
- VPN technology is used where necessary.  
- When data storage media, data and printouts are no longer required, they are securely deleted or destroyed. This ensures, as far as possible, that the data cannot be recovered.  
- Where necessary, data transfers are logged.

*Input control*

Measures to ensure that it is possible to subsequently verify and determine whether, when and by whom personal data has been entered, amended or deleted in data processing systems:

- High standards in the legally compliant drafting of contracts with data processors regarding the processing of personal data, which include provisions on audit trails.  
- Use of logging and log analysis systems to document user inputs. Where changes are made to systems that process personal data, these are recorded and retained in accordance with regulations (e.g. in the form of log files).  
- The logic of data input and output is checked (verification of file paths, etc.).  
- Information is obtained from service providers regarding the measures taken to implement data protection requirements.  
- Verbal instructions are confirmed in writing.

*Integrity of consent data (state of the art – see Article 25(4) of the GDPR)*

Measures to ensure that consent records cannot be altered without detection and that their authenticity can be verified retrospectively:

- Each consent record is secured at the time of its creation with a cryptographic digital signature (ECDSA, SHA-384), using an asymmetric key stored in a hardware security module from which the private key cannot be extracted. The signature covers the entire record, so that any subsequent alteration – whether to the date, the domain or the consent decision – renders it invalid and is therefore detectable. The signature of each record can be re-verified at any time; a record whose signature cannot be verified is treated as invalid.

**3\. Ensuring availability (Article 32(1)(b) of the GDPR)**

Measures to ensure that personal data is protected against accidental destruction or loss.

*Specific measures for our production environment and associated systems*

Law & Innovation does not operate its own server resources in its own data centres. Where processing is carried out by processors, the following measures, amongst others, apply both before and during data processing:

- Monitoring/control of system activities by our staff.  
- Our production environment is backed up at regular intervals, or data mirroring procedures are used.  
- Hardware (in particular servers) is taken out of service following a check of the data storage media used therein and, where necessary, after the relevant data records have been backed up.  
- The systems are protected by an uninterruptible power supply (UPS).  
- A multi-layered virus protection and firewall architecture is in place.  
- The data centres used are equipped with fire, water and temperature early-warning systems in the server rooms, as well as fire doors.  
- Regular patch management.  
- Load balancing.  
- Data storage takes place as part of dynamic processes.  
- Penetration and load tests are carried out regularly.  
- The capacity limit for each data processing system is set above the required minimum before data processing begins.  
- Regular training for staff involved. AWS resources are used for the production system and associated systems.

*Further measures*

- Where companies are commissioned to process personal data, this is always subject to an existing data processing agreement that complies with the requirements of Article 28 of the GDPR. Standard form contracts are provided for this purpose. These also ensure that Law & Innovation is informed at an early stage of any potential threats to availability.  
- Use of antivirus software on employees' computers.  
- The storage of data on employees' computers is minimised as far as possible. Data is stored on secure cloud systems.  
- Standard software used is subject to prior vetting and may only be obtained from limited, secure sources.  
- Contingency plans with specific instructions have been drawn up for security and data protection incidents.

**4\. Ensuring recoverability (Article 32(1)(b) of the GDPR)**

In the event of a physical or technical incident, measures are in place to ensure rapid recovery and, as part of an action plan, go beyond mere data backup. In order to be able to restore ongoing operations in these disaster scenarios, the following measures are taken:

*Specific measures for our production environment (CMP) and associated systems*

- Daily backup of all server resources by the hosting provider (AWS).  
- Disaster recovery.  
- Conclusion of Service Level Agreements (SLAs) with service providers.  
- Multi-tier backup procedures.  
- Redundant storage (cluster configurations / geo-redundancy) of data (e.g. disk mirroring).  
- Use of firewalls, IDS/IPS.  
- Fire and fire-suppression water protection.  
- Alarm monitoring.  
- Failure, emergency and recovery plans and scenarios.

**5\. Measures for the pseudonymisation of personal data**

Pseudonymisation is the processing of personal data in such a way that the personal data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that this additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organisational measures that ensure the personal data is not attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person. To this end, the following measures are taken:

- Introduction of a strict 'data protection by design' approach.  
- Drawing up a pseudonymisation plan (including a definition of the data to be replaced, pseudonymisation rules and a description of the procedure).

**6\. Procedures for the regular review, assessment and evaluation of the effectiveness of the technical and organisational measures**

Regular review, assessment and evaluation of the effectiveness of the technical and organisational measures to ensure the secure processing of personal data are carried out through the following measures:

- **Data Protection Management System:** All procedures, requests from authorities, contracts and registers are retained for documentation and transparency purposes. Changes are also documented.  
- **Information Security Management System:** All policies, processes and risk analyses are maintained within an internal ISMS.  
- **Data processing on behalf of Law & Innovation or by data processors:** The appointment of a data processor is always preceded by a comprehensive selection process and a preliminary assessment. In particular, we check whether potential data processors also meet the high standards described here. Processing may only take place once this has been done and a data processing agreement has been concluded that complies with the requirements of Article 28 of the GDPR. In addition to the preliminary assessment, we carry out regular audits to ensure that the required standard is maintained at all times. The agreed services are set out in detail in the data processing agreements in order to clearly define the scope of the contract.  
- **Staff training and awareness-raising:** At the start of their employment with Law & Innovation, all staff members receive all relevant information on data protection and information security and are bound by a duty of confidentiality. Through regular (refresher) training and the provision of targeted information (articles, case studies, etc.), we ensure a consistently high level of awareness amongst staff.  
- **Up-to-date security policy:** The security policy is reviewed regularly and updated as necessary.  
- **Responsibilities:** Responsibility for implementing the measures and processes described here lies with the relevant departments or specialist units. Regular monitoring is carried out in part by the Data Protection Officer and the Information Security Officer.  
- **Further measures:** Review of information on newly identified vulnerabilities and other risk factors, including a revision of the risk analysis and assessment where necessary.
